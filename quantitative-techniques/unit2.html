2.1 Data interpretation (DI)

DI questions are mostly percentages, ratios and averages (Unit I) applied to data shown in a table or chart. The skill is reading the right numbers quickly and approximating smartly.

How to approach any DI set

A DI set gives one data display and 4–6 questions on it. Each question needs one or two numbers from the display and a quick calculation.
Every placement test, bank/SSC exam, CAT; also real-life reading of business reports and dashboards.
  1. Read the title, units and legends first (₹ crore? '000 units? percentages of what total?).
  2. Read the question; identify exactly which values are needed.
  3. Approximate when options are far apart; compute exactly only when they are close.
  4. For "percentage change" always divide by the earlier/base value.
  5. Watch for traps: % of total vs absolute value; "approximately"; different units in one chart.
A chart read wrongly gives a confidently wrong answer — always re-check the scale.

C. Pie charts

Reading a pie chart

A pie shows parts of a whole (100 % = 360°). Each sector's central angle = (percentage × 360)/100 = 3.6° per 1 %.
Budgets, market share, composition of expenses or population.
Value of a sector = (sector % / 100) × total, or (angle/360) × total.
You cannot compare two pies' sectors directly if their totals differ — convert to values first.
  1. Central angle for Rent? 25 × 3.6 = 90°.
  2. Amount spent on Education? 15 % of 60,000 = ₹9,000.
  3. By how much does Food exceed Transport? (30 − 5) % of 60,000 = ₹15,000.
  4. Savings as a % of Food? 17/30 × 100 ≈ 56.7 %.
  5. If income rises 10 % and the savings share stays the same, new savings? 17 % of 66,000 = ₹11,220.

D. Line graphs

  1. In which year did Y earn more than X? 2022 (28 vs 22).
  2. Total profit of X over 5 years? 20 + 25 + 22 + 30 + 35 = ₹132 lakh; Y = ₹121 lakh.
  3. % increase in Y's profit from 2020 to 2024? (32 − 15)/15 × 100 ≈ 113.3 %.
  4. In which year was the gap between X and Y the largest? Gaps: 5, 5, 6, 4, 3 → 2022.
  5. Average profit of X? 132/5 = ₹26.4 lakh.

E. Data sufficiency

Data sufficiency (DS)

A DS question asks whether the given statements provide enough information to answer — not for the answer itself. Standard options:
  1. Statement I alone is sufficient, II alone is not.
  2. Statement II alone is sufficient, I alone is not.
  3. Each statement alone is sufficient.
  4. Both together are sufficient, neither alone is.
  5. Both together are not sufficient.
Tests logical reasoning about data — common in bank, MBA and placement tests; mirrors real decisions ("do we have enough data to decide?").
  1. Check statement I alone — can you get a unique answer?
  2. Check statement II alone (forget I).
  3. Only if neither works alone, combine them.
  4. Don't fully solve — stop once you know a unique answer exists.
The trap is using information from one statement while judging the other, or assuming facts not given (e.g. that x is positive).
  1. What is x? I: 2x + 3 = 11. II: x² = 16. → I gives x = 4 (unique); II gives ±4 (not unique) → A.
  2. What is the speed of the train? I: it crosses a pole in 10 s. II: its length is 200 m. → Neither alone; together 200/10 = 20 m/s → D.
  3. Is the integer n even? I: n² is even. II: n + 1 is odd. → Each alone proves n even → C.
  4. What is A's age? I: A is 5 years older than B. II: B is half as old as C. → Still three unknowns → E.
  1. What is the profit %? I: CP is ₹400. II: SP is ₹500.
    Show answer
    D — both needed (25 %).
  2. What is the area of the rectangle? I: perimeter is 30 cm. II: the diagonal is 13 cm.
    Show answer
    l + b = 15, l² + b² = 169 → 2lb = 225 − 169 = 56 → area 28. Both needed → D.
  3. Is x > y? I: x − y = 3. II: x = y + 3.
    Show answer
    Each alone gives x = y + 3 → C.

2.2 Permutations and combinations

Fundamental principles of counting

Multiplication (AND) rule: if one task can be done in m ways and, after it, another in n ways, both together can be done in m × n ways. Addition (OR) rule: if a task can be done in m ways OR (exclusively) in n ways, it can be done in m + n ways.
The basis of every counting question: outfits, passwords, routes, number plates.
3 shirts and 4 trousers → 3 × 4 = 12 outfits. Travel by one of 3 buses or 2 trains → 3 + 2 = 5 ways. A 4-digit PIN with digits 0–9 → 10⁴ = 10,000.
The addition rule needs the options to be mutually exclusive.

Factorial and permutations (arrangements — order matters)

n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1; 0! = 1. A permutation is an arrangement in which order matters (ABC ≠ BCA).
ⁿPr = n!(n − r)! (r of n distinct items, no repetition)    nr (with repetition) Identical items: n!p! q! r! …    Circular: (n − 1)!    Necklace/garland: (n − 1)!/2
Arranging people in seats, forming numbers and words, ranking, scheduling.
  1. 4-digit numbers from 1–9 without repetition: ⁹P₄ = 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 3,024; with repetition 9⁴ = 6,561.
  2. Arrangements of LEADER (E twice): 6!/2! = 360. MISSISSIPPI: 11!/(4! 4! 2!) = 34,650.
  3. INDIA with vowels together: vowels I, I, A form one block → units N, D, [IIA] → 3! = 6; inside the block 3!/2! = 3 → 18 (out of 5!/2! = 60).
  4. 6 people around a round table: (6 − 1)! = 120; 6 beads on a necklace: 5!/2 = 60.
  5. 5 boys and 3 girls in a row, no two girls together: arrange boys 5! = 120; 6 gaps for girls → ⁶P₃ = 120 → 14,400.
Forgetting to divide for identical items, or treating circular arrangements like linear ones, are common errors.

Combinations (selections — order does not matter)

A combination is a selection where order does not matter ({A, B, C} = {C, A, B}).
ⁿCr = n!r! (n − r)! = ⁿPrr!    ⁿCr = ⁿCn−r    ⁿC₀ = ⁿCn = 1    ⁿCr + ⁿCr−1 = ⁿ⁺¹Cr
Committees, teams, choosing questions in an exam, lottery, handshakes, lines through points.
  1. ¹⁰C₃ = 10 × 9 × 8/(3 × 2 × 1) = 120. ¹⁰C₈ = ¹⁰C₂ = 45 (use the smaller r).
  2. Committee of 3 men and 2 women from 6 men and 5 women: ⁶C₃ × ⁵C₂ = 20 × 10 = 200.
  3. Committee of 4 from 5 men and 4 women with at least one woman: total − none = ⁹C₄ − ⁵C₄ = 126 − 5 = 121.
  4. Handshakes among 10 people: ¹⁰C₂ = 45. Diagonals of a hexagon: ⁶C₂ − 6 = 9.
  5. Choose 5 questions out of 8 when Q1 is compulsory: ⁷C₄ = 35.
Ask "does order matter?" — if swapping two chosen items gives a different outcome, use permutations.
  1. In how many ways can the letters of BANANA be arranged?
    Show answer
    6!/(3! 2!) = 60 (A ×3, N ×2).
  2. How many 3-digit even numbers can be formed from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 without repetition?
    Show answer
    Units digit 2 or 4 (2 ways) × 4 × 3 = 24.
  3. A team of 11 is chosen from 15 players. In how many ways if a particular player must be included?
    Show answer
    ¹⁴C₁₀ = ¹⁴C₄ = 1,001.
  4. How many triangles can be formed from 10 points, no three collinear?
    Show answer
    ¹⁰C₃ = 120.

2.3 Probability (basics)

Basic probability

Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain): P(E) = number of favourable outcomestotal number of equally likely outcomes Key terms: experiment, outcome, sample space, event, mutually exclusive, independent (see the Data Analytics notes for the full theory).
Games, lotteries, risk, quality control, insurance.
  • Complement: P(not E) = 1 − P(E). "At least one" → 1 − P(none).
  • Addition: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
  • Independent events: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • Odds in favour a : b → P = a/(a + b).
Sample spaces: one coin 2, two coins 4, three coins 8 (2ⁿ); one die 6, two dice 36; a pack has 52 cards (4 suits × 13; 26 red, 26 black; 12 face cards; 4 aces).
The classical formula needs equally likely outcomes — "win or lose" is not 50–50!
  1. Two dice: P(sum = 9)? (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) → 4/36 = 1/9.
  2. Three coins: P(at least one head)? 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
  3. A card is drawn: P(face card)? 12/52 = 3/13. P(red king) = 2/52 = 1/26.
  4. Bag: 4 red, 5 blue. Two balls drawn. P(both blue)? ⁵C₂/⁹C₂ = 10/36 = 5/18. P(one of each) = (4 × 5)/36 = 5/9.
  5. A leap year has 366 days = 52 weeks + 2 days. P(53 Sundays)? The 2 extra days are one of 7 equally likely pairs (Sun–Mon, …, Sat–Sun); 2 contain Sunday → 2/7.
  1. A number is chosen from 1 to 50. P(it is a multiple of 3 or 5)?
    Show answer
    16 + 10 − 3 = 23 → 23/50.
  2. P(getting a doublet when two dice are thrown)?
    Show answer
    6/36 = 1/6.
  3. A and B solve a problem with probabilities ½ and ⅓. P(the problem is solved)?
    Show answer
    1 − (½)(⅔) = 2/3.

2.4 Logarithms (basics)

Definition

A logarithm is an exponent. If aˣ = N (a > 0, a ≠ 1, N > 0), then loga N = x — "the power to which a must be raised to give N". Common log: base 10 (log); natural log: base e ≈ 2.718 (ln).
Solving exponential equations (compound interest time, population growth), number of digits of huge numbers, decibel/Richter/pH scales, algorithm complexity in computing (log₂ n).
log₂ 32 = 5 (2⁵ = 32); log₁₀ 1000 = 3; log₁₀ 0.001 = −3; log₅ 1 = 0; log₇ 7 = 1.
log of zero or a negative number is undefined (for real numbers); the base cannot be 1.

Laws of logarithms

loga(mn) = loga m + loga n    loga(m/n) = loga m − loga n    loga(mⁿ) = n loga m loga a = 1    loga 1 = 0    aloga x = x    logb a = log alog b (change of base)    loga b × logb a = 1
Turn multiplication into addition and powers into multiplication — that is why logs were invented (log tables before calculators).
  1. Given log 2 = 0.3010, log 3 = 0.4771: log 12 = 2 log 2 + log 3 = 1.0791; log 5 = log 10 − log 2 = 0.6990; log 1.5 = log 3 − log 2 = 0.1761.
  2. Solve logx 81 = 4: x⁴ = 81 → x = 3.
  3. Solve log(x + 1) + log(x − 1) = log 3: log(x² − 1) = log 3 → x² = 4 → x = 2 (x = −2 rejected: log of a negative).
  4. Number of digits in 2⁵⁰: log 2⁵⁰ = 50 × 0.3010 = 15.05 → characteristic 15 → 16 digits.
  5. Simplify log 8 + log 25 = log 200? No: log(8 × 25) = log 200 ≈ 2.301. And log₂ 8 × log₈ 2 = 1.
log(m + n) ≠ log m + log n — a frequent mistake.
  1. Find log₃ 243 and log₄ (1/16).
    Show answer
    3⁵ = 243 → 5; 4⁻² = 1/16 → −2.
  2. If log 2 = 0.3010, find log 32 and log 0.125.
    Show answer
    log 32 = 5 × 0.3010 = 1.505; log 0.125 = log (1/8) = −3 × 0.3010 = −0.903.
  3. Solve 2ˣ = 64 and 3^(x − 1) = 27.
    Show answer
    x = 6; x − 1 = 3 → x = 4.

2.5 Sequences and series: AP and GP

Arithmetic progression (AP)

A sequence in which each term is obtained by adding a fixed number d (common difference) to the previous term: a, a + d, a + 2d, … (3, 7, 11, 15, … with d = 4). A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence.
nth term Tn = a + (n − 1)d    Sn = n2[2a + (n − 1)d] = n2(first + last) Arithmetic mean of a and b = a + b2    number of terms n = last − firstd + 1
Salary with fixed annual increments, EMI-like instalments, seats in rows of a theatre, savings increasing by a fixed amount.
  1. 20th term and sum of 20 terms of 3, 7, 11, …: T₂₀ = 3 + 19 × 4 = 79; S₂₀ = 10 × (3 + 79) = 820.
  2. How many terms in 7, 11, …, 99? (99 − 7)/4 + 1 = 24.
  3. Sum of all 2-digit multiples of 3: 12, 15, …, 99 → n = 30 → 15 × (12 + 99) = 1,665.
  4. Insert 3 arithmetic means between 2 and 18: 5 terms → d = (18 − 2)/4 = 4 → 6, 10, 14.
  5. Salary ₹20,000 rising by ₹1,500 each year: 10th year = 20,000 + 9 × 1,500 = ₹33,500; total over 10 years = 5 × (20,000 + 33,500) = ₹2,67,500.
Only for constant differences — growth by a percentage is a GP, not an AP.

Geometric progression (GP)

Each term is obtained by multiplying by a fixed number r (common ratio): a, ar, ar², … (2, 6, 18, 54, … with r = 3).
Tn = arⁿ⁻¹    Sn = a(rⁿ − 1)r − 1 (r ≠ 1)    S∞ = a1 − r (|r| < 1)    GM of a, b = √(ab)
Compound interest, population growth, depreciation, bacteria doubling, recurring decimals.
  1. 8th term of 2, 6, 18, …: 2 × 3⁷ = 4,374; sum of 6 terms = 2(3⁶ − 1)/2 = 728.
  2. 1 + ½ + ¼ + … to infinity = 1/(1 − ½) = 2.
  3. 0.333… as a fraction: 0.3 + 0.03 + … = 0.3/(1 − 0.1) = 1/3.
  4. GM of 4 and 16 = √64 = 8 (AM = 10; note AM ≥ GM).
  5. Bacteria double every hour, starting with 100: after 10 hours 100 × 2¹⁰ = 1,02,400.
The infinite sum exists only when |r| < 1.

A harmonic progression is a sequence whose reciprocals form an AP (1, ½, ⅓, …). For positive a, b: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM, and GM² = AM × HM. The average speed for equal distances, 2xy/(x + y), is the harmonic mean!

  1. Which term of the AP 5, 9, 13, … is 101?
    Show answer
    5 + (n − 1)4 = 101 → n = 25.
  2. Find the sum of the first 50 odd numbers.
    Show answer
    n² = 2,500.
  3. The 3rd term of a GP is 12 and the 6th term is 96. Find a and r.
    Show answer
    r³ = 96/12 = 8 → r = 2; a = 12/4 = 3.
  4. Sum to infinity of 8 + 4 + 2 + …
    Show answer
    8/(1 − ½) = 16.

2.6 Geometry (basics): areas and volumes

  • Complementary angles add to 90°; supplementary add to 180°; angles on a straight line add to 180°, around a point to 360°.
  • Vertically opposite angles are equal.
  • When a transversal cuts two parallel lines: corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, co-interior angles add to 180°.
  • Triangle: angles add to 180°; an exterior angle = sum of the two opposite interior angles.
  • Polygon with n sides: interior angles add to (n − 2) × 180°; each exterior angle of a regular polygon = 360°/n (hexagon: 120° interior, 60° exterior).

2.7 Problem-solving strategies

Approximation techniques

When options are not close together, replace awkward numbers with nearby round ones and estimate — the nearest option is the answer.
DI calculations, simplification, percentage questions under time pressure.
  • 4,987 × 1.98 ≈ 5,000 × 2 = 10,000 → pick 9,874.26 from options 8,640, 9,874.26, 11,200, 12,100.
  • 49.9 % of 801 ≈ 50 % of 800 = 400.
  • √1520 ≈ 39 (39² = 1521); ∛9260 ≈ 21 (21³ = 9261).
  • Fractions instead of %: 37.5 % of 640 = 3/8 × 640 = 240; 16.66 % = 1/6; 14.28 % = 1/7.
  • Round in opposite directions to cancel errors: 298 × 52 ≈ 300 × 50 = 15,000 (actual 15,496).
Do not approximate when options differ by less than about 5 %; compute exactly then.

Time-saving tricks

Small habits that save seconds on every question.
All timed aptitude tests.
  • Learn tables up to 20, squares up to 30, cubes up to 15, fraction–% equivalents (Unit I).
  • Use the LCM/unit method in work and pipe problems instead of fractions.
  • Use successive % (a + b + ab/100) for compound changes, areas and discounts.
  • Use ratios instead of actual values where possible (speed ratio ↔ time ratio).
  • Attempt in rounds: easy questions first, mark and return to time-consuming ones.
  • Write the given data neatly; convert units first (km/h → m/s).
Tricks without understanding fail on twisted questions — learn the method, then the trick.

Elimination methods

In multiple-choice tests you can often rule out wrong options without solving fully.
Speed and accuracy — also a check on your own answer.
  • Unit-digit check: 347 × 26 must end in 2 (7 × 6 = 42) → eliminate options not ending in 2.
  • Divisibility check: if the answer must be a multiple of 9 (digit sum), discard others.
  • Range / sign check: a probability must be between 0 and 1; a discounted price must be below MP; an average lies between min and max.
  • Back-solving (plugging in options): "a number when increased by 20 % becomes 96" → try the options: 80 × 1.2 = 96 ✓.
  • Parity (odd/even): sum of two odd numbers is even, etc.
  • Extreme values: test with simple numbers (e.g. assume total = 100) to check a formula.
Elimination narrows choices; if two options survive, compute properly.
