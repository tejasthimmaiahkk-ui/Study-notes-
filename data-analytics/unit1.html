1.0 Foundations: data, information and knowledge

Before "analytics" we need a clear idea of what data is and the forms it takes. This section fills that gap.

Data, information, knowledge, wisdom (DIKW)

Data are raw facts and figures with no context ("37", "Bengaluru", "2025-03-14"). Information is data organised and given context ("37 °C was the maximum temperature in Bengaluru on 14 March 2025"). Knowledge is information combined with experience to understand patterns ("March temperatures have risen 2 °C in ten years"). Wisdom is knowing what to do ("install more cooling capacity before summer"). Analytics is the process that climbs this pyramid.
A retailer's billing records (data) → daily sales report (information) → "sales dip every monsoon" (knowledge) → "run a monsoon discount campaign" (wisdom/decision).
Each step adds processing: cleaning and organising (data → information), analysis and pattern finding (information → knowledge), judgement and context (knowledge → wisdom).
Poor-quality data at the bottom ruins every level above ("garbage in, garbage out").

Quantitative data are discrete (countable: number of customers) or continuous (measurable: time, weight). Sources are primary (collected by you: surveys, experiments, sensors) or secondary (already existing: government data, company databases, web).

The measurement scale decides which statistic you may use: e.g. Pearson's r needs interval/ratio data, Spearman's rank works on ordinal data (Unit II), Chi-square works on nominal counts (Unit III).

1.2 Data analytics overview

What is data analytics?

Data analytics is the science of examining raw data — collecting, cleaning, transforming and modelling it — to discover useful information, draw conclusions and support decision-making. It combines statistics, computing and business knowledge.
Netflix recommending shows, banks flagging fraudulent card swipes, Swiggy estimating delivery time, a college analysing which subjects students fail in most.
Uses tools such as Excel, SQL, Python/R, Power BI/Tableau, and techniques from descriptive statistics to machine learning.
Analytics shows correlation, not automatically causation; results depend on data quality and on asking the right question.
TermFocus
Data analysisThe act of inspecting a particular data set (one step).
Data analyticsThe whole discipline and process — including tools, techniques and acting on results.
Business intelligence (BI)Reporting and dashboards on historical data, mostly descriptive (Unit IV).
Data scienceBroader: analytics + programming + machine learning + building data products.

The data analytics process (lifecycle)

  1. Define the objective — a clear, measurable business question ("Why did repeat purchases fall 10 % in Q2?").
  2. Collect data — internal (sales, CRM, ERP) and external (surveys, social media, government data).
  3. Clean and prepare — remove duplicates and errors, handle missing values, fix formats, combine sources (Power Query — Unit IV).
  4. Analyse — descriptive statistics, correlation/regression (Unit II), hypothesis tests (Unit III), machine-learning models.
  5. Interpret and visualise — charts and dashboards that tell a clear story.
  6. Act and monitor — make the decision, measure its effect, repeat.

The industry-standard process model (1996): Business understanding → Data understanding → Data preparation → Modelling → Evaluation → Deployment, with feedback loops. It maps almost one-to-one to the six steps above.

1.3 Types of data analytics

Analytics is classified by the question it answers. Each type builds on the previous one and adds both value and difficulty (Gartner's analytics ascendancy model).

1. Descriptive analytics — "What happened?"

Summarises historical data to show what has happened. It is the foundation of all analytics and the most common type (≈ 80 % of business reporting).
Monthly sales report; number of website visitors last week; average marks per subject; a Power BI dashboard of revenue by region; KPIs such as profit margin and churn rate.
Techniques: aggregation (sum, count, average), measures of central tendency and dispersion (mean, median, standard deviation), percentages and growth rates, data visualisation (bar, line, pie charts), reports and dashboards. Tools: Excel, SQL, Power BI, Tableau.
Tells what but not why; looks backward only; can hide detail behind averages.

2. Diagnostic analytics — "Why did it happen?"

Digs into the data to find causes and relationships behind what descriptive analytics showed.
Sales fell in March — drilling down shows the fall is in one region, where a competitor opened a store; a hospital investigating why readmissions rose; finding that delivery delays correlate with rainfall.
Techniques: drill-down and drill-through (Unit IV), data discovery, correlation and regression (Unit II), root-cause analysis, comparison of segments, hypothesis testing (Unit III).
Correlation ≠ causation; needs domain knowledge to interpret; limited to explaining the past.

3. Predictive analytics — "What is likely to happen?"

Uses historical patterns with statistical and machine-learning models to forecast future outcomes and estimate probabilities.
Sales/demand forecasting for festive seasons; credit scoring (probability a borrower defaults); predicting customer churn; predicting machine failure; weather forecasts; IPL score prediction.
Techniques: regression (Unit II), time-series forecasting, classification (decision trees, logistic regression), probability distributions (Unit III), neural networks. Tools: Python (scikit-learn), R, Azure ML, Power BI forecasting.
Predictions are probabilities, not certainties; assume the future resembles the past (failed during COVID-19); models can be biased by biased data.

4. Prescriptive analytics — "What should we do?"

Recommends the best action to achieve a goal, by combining predictions with optimisation and simulation of alternative decisions.
Airline dynamic pricing; Google Maps choosing the fastest route; inventory reorder quantities; Amazon's product recommendations; optimal staff rostering in hospitals.
Techniques: optimisation (linear programming), simulation (Monte Carlo), decision trees/analysis, recommendation engines, reinforcement learning, business rules.
Most complex and expensive; needs high-quality data and accurate predictive models; recommendations must still be checked by humans (ethics, context).

1.4 Importance and benefits of data analytics

Why organisations invest in analytics

Analytics replaces gut feeling with evidence: data-driven decision making.
See the benefits below — each is a heading you can use in an answer.
  1. Better, faster decisions — managers see facts in real-time dashboards.
  2. Understanding customers — segmentation, preferences, lifetime value → personalised marketing.
  3. Operational efficiency and cost reduction — find bottlenecks, optimise inventory and routes (e.g. UPS saves millions of litres of fuel through route analytics).
  4. Increased revenue — pricing optimisation, cross-selling, demand forecasting.
  5. Risk management and fraud detection — spotting unusual transactions in real time.
  6. Competitive advantage — spotting market trends earlier than competitors.
  7. Product and service improvement — feature usage and feedback analysis.
  8. Performance measurement — KPIs and goals tracked objectively.
Challenges: poor data quality, data silos, privacy and security (India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023), shortage of skilled people, cost of tools, and misinterpretation of results.

1.6 Text analytics

What text analytics is

Text analytics (text mining) is the process of converting unstructured text — reviews, e-mails, tweets, chat logs, documents — into structured data and insights using natural language processing (NLP), statistics and machine learning.
Sentiment of product reviews on Amazon/Flipkart, classifying customer-support tickets, detecting spam, analysing brand mentions on social media, summarising legal documents, resume screening, chatbots.
  1. Collect text — reviews, surveys, social-media APIs, documents.
  2. Pre-process: lower-casing; tokenisation (split into words); remove punctuation and stop words ("the", "is", "and"); stemming (cut words to a root: "running" → "run") or lemmatisation (dictionary form: "better" → "good").
  3. Represent as numbers — bag of words (word counts), TF-IDF (term frequency × inverse document frequency: words frequent in this document but rare overall get high weight), word embeddings.
  4. Analyse: sentiment analysis (positive/negative/neutral), topic modelling (themes), named-entity recognition (people, places, brands), classification and clustering, keyword frequency / word clouds.
  5. Visualise and act — dashboards of sentiment over time, alerts for complaints.
Sarcasm, slang, emojis, spelling errors and mixed languages (Hinglish/Kanglish) confuse algorithms; context and ambiguity ("this phone is sick!"); needs large labelled data for accuracy; privacy concerns.

1.7 Web analytics

What web analytics is

Web analytics is the collection, measurement, analysis and reporting of website and app usage data to understand and optimise visitor behaviour. Two kinds: on-site (behaviour on your own site — Google Analytics, Microsoft Clarity) and off-site (your audience and reputation across the web — social mentions, search visibility).
An e-commerce site finds that 70 % of users abandon the cart at the payment page; a news site sees which articles keep readers longest; a college website checks which pages admission seekers visit.

How data is collected: a JavaScript page tag sends an event for every page view/click; cookies identify returning visitors; server log files record requests; UTM parameters tag campaign links.

Key metrics:

MetricMeaning / formula

Process: define goals and KPIs → collect data → report → analyse (funnels, segments) → test changes (A/B testing: show two versions to random halves and compare conversion) → implement.

Ad-blockers and cookie consent hide part of the traffic; bots inflate numbers; cannot explain why users behave as they do (needs surveys/usability tests); privacy regulations (GDPR, DPDP Act).

1.8 Skills for business analytics

The analyst's skill set

A business analyst sits between data and decision-makers, so needs three groups of skills: technical, analytical/business and soft skills.
Job roles: data analyst, business analyst, BI developer, MIS executive, marketing analyst, data scientist.
GroupSkills
TechnicalExcel (pivot tables, lookups), SQL (querying databases), a programming language (Python with pandas, or R), BI tools (Power BI, Tableau), data cleaning, basic machine learning, cloud platforms, data modelling
Statistical / analyticalDescriptive statistics, probability, correlation & regression, hypothesis testing, critical thinking, problem solving, attention to detail
BusinessDomain knowledge (finance, retail, HR …), understanding KPIs, framing business questions, project management
Soft skillsCommunication and data storytelling (explaining results to non-technical managers), visualisation design, teamwork, curiosity, ethics and data privacy awareness
Tools change quickly — continuous learning is itself a required skill.
