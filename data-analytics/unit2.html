2.0 Foundation: the statistics you need first

Every formula in this unit uses Σ (sigma) notation, means, deviations and standard deviations. If any of these feel shaky, work through this section carefully — it makes the rest mechanical.

Σ notation and the arithmetic mean

Σ (capital sigma) means "add up". ΣX = X₁ + X₂ + … + XN. ΣX² means square each value then add; (ΣX)² means add first then square — they are different! The arithmetic mean is X̄ = ΣX / N.
The mean is the "centre" from which we measure how far each value deviates.
X = 4, 8, 6, 2, 10: ΣX = 30, N = 5, X̄ = 6. ΣX² = 16 + 64 + 36 + 4 + 100 = 220, but (ΣX)² = 900.
The mean is pulled by extreme values (outliers).

Deviation, variance and standard deviation

A deviation is the distance of a value from the mean: x = X − X̄ (lowercase x is the standard notation for a deviation). Deviations always sum to zero: Σx = 0. To measure spread we square them: variance σ² = Σx²/N and standard deviation σ = √(Σx²/N).
σ measures how spread out data are; σx and σy appear in the correlation and regression formulas.

Bivariate data, covariance and the scatter diagram

Bivariate data are pairs (X, Y) measured on the same items (height and weight of each student). The covariance measures how they vary together: Cov(X, Y) = Σxy / N, where xy is the product of the two deviations. Positive when both tend to be above (or below) their means together; negative when one is above while the other is below.
The scatter diagram — plotting each pair as a point — is the first, visual method of studying correlation.
If points rise from left to right → positive correlation; fall → negative; no pattern → no correlation; points close to a straight line → strong.
Covariance depends on units (₹ vs thousands of ₹), so its size is not comparable — we standardise it by dividing by σxσy, which gives Pearson's r.

2.1 Introduction, meaning and types of correlation

Meaning of correlation

Correlation is a statistical technique that measures the degree and direction of the relationship between two (or more) variables. The coefficient of correlation (r) is a number between −1 and +1 summarising it.
Definition (Croxton & Cowden): "When the relationship is of a quantitative nature, the appropriate statistical tool for discovering and measuring the relationship and expressing it in a brief formula is known as correlation."
Price and demand; advertising spend and sales; study hours and marks; rainfall and crop yield; temperature and ice-cream sales.
Methods of studying correlation: (1) scatter diagram, (2) Karl Pearson's coefficient, (3) Spearman's rank correlation, (4) concurrent deviation (OOS).
Correlation does not imply causation. Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are correlated because both rise in summer (a third variable). Correlation may also be spurious (purely by chance).

2.2 Karl Pearson's coefficient of correlation (individual series)

Definition and formulas

Karl Pearson (1896) defined the coefficient of correlation as the covariance divided by the product of the standard deviations — the most widely used measure of linear correlation. Also called the product-moment correlation coefficient.
r = Cov(X, Y)σx σy = ΣxyN σx σy
Measuring the strength of linear relations between quantitative variables (interval/ratio scale) — marks vs study hours, sales vs advertising.
Three computational methods give the same r:
r = ΣxyΣx² × Σy²   where x = X − X̄, y = Y − Ȳ
r = NΣXY − ΣX·ΣYNΣX² − (ΣX)² × NΣY² − (ΣY)²
r = NΣdxdy − Σdx·ΣdyNΣdx² − (Σdx)² × NΣdy² − (Σdy)²   dx = X − A, dy = Y − B
Use Method 1 when the means are whole numbers, Method 3 when they are fractions, Method 2 when values are small.
Measures only linear relationships (r can be ≈ 0 for a perfect U-shaped relation); strongly affected by outliers; assumes both variables are quantitative and roughly normally distributed; time-consuming by hand.

Properties of Pearson's r

  1. −1 ≤ r ≤ +1. r = +1 perfect positive, −1 perfect negative, 0 no linear correlation.
  2. Independent of change of origin and scale: if U = (X − A)/h and V = (Y − B)/k (h, k > 0), then rUV = rXY. (That is why Method 3 works.)
  3. r is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients: r = ±√(byx × bxy) (Section 2.6).
  4. Symmetric: rxy = ryx.
  5. r is a pure number — no units.
  6. If X and Y are independent, r = 0 (but r = 0 does not prove independence — the relation may be non-linear).
  7. r² is the coefficient of determination: the proportion of variation in Y explained by X (r = 0.9 → 81 %).
Properties 2 and 3 are favourite 2–5 mark questions; property 7 is used to interpret regression models.
Example of property 2: multiplying all X values by 10 (₹ → paise) does not change r.
If h and k have opposite signs, the sign of r flips.

2.3 Probable error of the coefficient of correlation

Probable error (P.E.)

r is usually computed from a sample. The probable error tells us how reliable r is — the range within which the population correlation (ρ) is likely to lie, and whether r is significant.
P.E.r = 0.6745 × 1 − r²√N      (S.E.r = 1 − r²√N, P.E. = 0.6745 × S.E.)
Deciding whether an observed correlation is real or due to chance; setting limits for the population coefficient.
Interpretation rules:
  1. If r < P.E. → correlation is not significant (no evidence of correlation).
  2. If r > 6 × P.E. → correlation is significant (definite).
  3. In between → nothing definite can be concluded.
  4. Limits of the population correlation: ρ = r ± P.E.
0.6745 is used because in a normal distribution 50 % of values lie within ±0.6745 standard errors of the mean.
Valid only when the sample is random, N is reasonably large, and data come from a normal population; it is an older method (t-tests on r are used today).
  1. r² = 0.8997; 1 − r² = 0.1003; √7 = 2.6458.
  2. P.E. = 0.6745 × 0.1003 / 2.6458 = 0.0256.
  3. 6 × P.E. = 0.1534. Since r = 0.9485 > 0.1534 → correlation is significant.
  4. Limits: 0.9485 ± 0.0256 → 0.9229 to 0.9741.
  1. r = 0.8, N = 25. Find P.E. and comment.
    Show answer
    P.E. = 0.6745 × 0.36/5 = 0.0486; 6 P.E. = 0.291 < 0.8 → significant. Limits 0.7514 to 0.8486.
  2. r = 0.2, N = 16. Is it significant?
    Show answer
    P.E. = 0.6745 × 0.96/4 = 0.1619; 6 P.E. = 0.971. r lies between P.E. and 6 P.E. → nothing definite (not significant).

2.4 Spearman's rank correlation (individual series)

Rank correlation coefficient

Developed by Charles Spearman (1904). Instead of the actual values, we use their ranks (positions). It measures how similar two rankings are — ideal for qualitative characteristics that can be ordered but not measured (beauty, honesty, taste, judges' preferences).
R = 1 − 6 ΣD²N(N² − 1)     D = R₁ − R₂ (difference of ranks)
Agreement between two judges in a competition; ranking of products by price vs quality; ranks of students in two subjects; market research on preferences.
Three cases:
  1. Ranks are given — compute D = R₁ − R₂, D², ΣD², apply the formula.
  2. Ranks not given (actual values given) — assign ranks: rank 1 to the highest value (or lowest — but use the same rule for both series), then proceed as case 1.
  3. Equal (tied) ranks — give each tied item the average of the ranks they would occupy (two items tied for 3rd and 4th each get 3.5). Then add a correction factor for every group of m tied items: R = 1 − 6 [ ΣD² + Σ (m³ − m)/12 ]N(N² − 1)
Check: ΣD must always be 0.
Loses information (only order, not size of differences); not suitable for grouped (frequency) data; becomes tedious for large N (> 30); with many ties the formula is approximate.

2.5 Introduction to regression

Meaning and definition

The word regression was used by Sir Francis Galton (1877): heights of sons of tall fathers tended to "regress" (go back) towards the average height. Today regression analysis means estimating the average relationship between variables so that the unknown value of one (the dependent variable) can be predicted from the known value of another (the independent variable).
Definition (M.M. Blair): "Regression analysis is a mathematical measure of the average relationship between two or more variables in terms of the original units of the data."
Predicting sales from advertising budget; estimating house price from area; forecasting demand from price; predicting marks from study hours; salary from experience.
A regression line is the line of best fit through the scatter of points. Simple linear regression uses one independent variable and a straight line Y = a + bX, where b (slope) = change in Y for a one-unit change in X, and a (intercept) = value of Y when X = 0.
Assumes a linear relationship that stays the same; predictions far outside the observed range (extrapolation) are unreliable; does not prove causation.

The method of least squares

Among all possible straight lines, the "best" is the one that makes the sum of the squared vertical distances (errors, residuals) between the points and the line as small as possible: minimise Σ(Y − Ŷ)².
Every regression calculator, Excel's trendline and Power BI's trend line use it.
For Y = a + bX, minimising gives two normal equations: ΣY = Na + bΣX      ΣXY = aΣX + bΣX² Solve them simultaneously for a and b. The line always passes through (X̄, Ȳ).
Squaring makes the method sensitive to outliers.

2.6 Regression equations and regression coefficients

Two regression lines

There are two regression lines (unless r = ±1):
  • Y on X: used to estimate Y for a given X (X independent). Minimises vertical errors.
  • X on Y: used to estimate X for a given Y. Minimises horizontal errors.
Y on X:   Y − Ȳ = byx(X − X̄),    byx = r σyσx = ΣxyΣx² X on Y:   X − X̄ = bxy(Y − Ȳ),    bxy = r σxσy = ΣxyΣy² With assumed means: byx = NΣdxdy − ΣdxΣdyNΣdx² − (Σdx)²
If a manager wants sales (Y) for a given advertising budget (X), use Y on X. To find the budget needed for a target sales figure, use X on Y.
Both lines intersect at the point of means (X̄, Ȳ). The closer |r| is to 1, the closer the two lines; if r = 0 they are perpendicular (Y = Ȳ and X = X̄).
Using the wrong line (e.g. Y on X to estimate X) gives a biased estimate.

Properties of regression coefficients

  1. r = ±√(byx × bxy) — r is the geometric mean of the two coefficients.
  2. Both coefficients and r have the same sign.
  3. If one coefficient is greater than 1, the other must be less than 1 (since byx·bxy = r² ≤ 1).
  4. The arithmetic mean of the coefficients is ≥ r: (byx + bxy)/2 ≥ r (for positive r).
  5. Coefficients are independent of change of origin but not of scale.
  6. The two lines pass through (X̄, Ȳ).
Used to check which given line is Y on X, and to find r and missing σ values.
Example: byx = 0.8, bxy = 0.45 → r = √0.36 = 0.6.
If a computed product byx·bxy exceeds 1, the assumption about which line is which was wrong.
