3.0 Foundation: sets and counting

Probability problems are counting problems in disguise. You need set operations (to combine events) and nCr (to count outcomes).

Sets and Venn diagrams

A set is a collection of distinct objects. For events A and B: A ∪ B (union) = A or B or both; A ∩ B (intersection) = both A and B; A′ or Ā (complement) = not A. A Venn diagram shows them as overlapping circles inside the sample space S.
"A king or a heart" is a union; "a red king" is an intersection; "not a six" is a complement.
n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B) — the overlap is subtracted because it was counted twice. This becomes the addition theorem of probability.
With three or more sets the inclusion–exclusion formula grows more terms.

Counting: factorial, permutations, combinations

n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 1 (0! = 1). The number of ways to choose r items from n when order does not matter: nCr = n! / [r!(n − r)!]. When order matters: nPr = n!/(n − r)!
Counting favourable outcomes: "2 cards from 52" = 52C2 = 1326; Binomial formula uses nCr.
5C2 = 5!/(2! 3!) = 10; 52C2 = 52 × 51 / 2 = 1326; 10C3 = 120.
See the Quantitative Techniques notes (P&C) for a full treatment.

3.3 Measures (approaches) of probability

1. Classical (a priori) approach

If an experiment has n equally likely, mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes and m of them are favourable to A: P(A) = mn = number of favourable outcomestotal number of outcomes
Games of chance — coins, dice, cards, lotteries.
P(even on a die) = 3/6 = 1/2. P(sum 8 with two dice) = 5/36 [(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)]. P(a king) = 4/52 = 1/13. Two balls from a bag of 5 red + 3 green, P(both red) = 5C2/8C2 = 10/28 = 5/14.
Needs equally likely outcomes and a finite sample space — fails for "probability that a machine fails" or "a person aged 30 dies this year".

2. Relative frequency (empirical, a posteriori) approach

Probability is the limit of the relative frequency when the experiment is repeated a very large number of times: P(A) = lim (m/n) as n → ∞.
Insurance (mortality tables), quality control (defect rates), weather forecasting, cricket statistics.
If 18 out of 600 bulbs were defective, P(defective) ≈ 18/600 = 0.03. (Try the dice simulator below: the relative frequency approaches 1/6.)
Requires many repetitions under identical conditions; gives only an estimate.

3. Subjective approach

Probability is a person's degree of belief, based on experience and judgement.
"There is a 70 % chance this product launch succeeds"; venture capital and management decisions where no data exist.
Experts may combine available evidence informally; Bayes' theorem (3.6) updates such beliefs with data.
Different people give different probabilities; can be biased.

4. Axiomatic approach (Kolmogorov, 1933)

Defines probability by three axioms that every approach above satisfies: (1) 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1; (2) P(S) = 1; (3) for mutually exclusive events, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
The mathematical foundation for all probability theory.
From the axioms: P(∅) = 0; P(A′) = 1 − P(A); if A ⊆ B then P(A) ≤ P(B).
Says how probabilities behave, not how to find their values.

Theorems of probability

Addition: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)    (mutually exclusive: P(A) + P(B)) Complement: P(A′) = 1 − P(A)     Multiplication: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)   (independent: P(A) × P(B))
  1. King or heart from a pack: P(K) = 4/52, P(H) = 13/52, P(K ∩ H) = 1/52 → P = (4 + 13 − 1)/52 = 16/52 = 4/13.
  2. At least one head in 3 tosses: P = 1 − P(no head) = 1 − (1/2)³ = 7/8 (complement trick — "at least one" problems).
  3. Independent: A hits a target with P = 0.6, B with 0.5. P(target is hit) = 1 − P(both miss) = 1 − 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.8.
  4. Dependent: two cards without replacement, both aces: (4/52) × (3/51) = 12/2652 = 1/221.

3.4 Conditional probability

Conditional probability P(A | B)

The probability of A given that B has already occurred. Knowing B shrinks the sample space to B: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B),   P(B) > 0 A and B are independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A).
P(customer buys | clicked the ad); P(disease | positive test); P(default | low credit score) — the core of predictive analytics.
Two dice: P(sum = 8 | first die shows 3) — only outcome (3,5) out of 6 → 1/6. Formula: P(sum 8 ∩ first 3) = 1/36, P(first 3) = 6/36 → (1/36)/(6/36) = 1/6 ✓.
P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A ∩ B) = 0.2: P(A | B) = 0.2/0.4 = 0.5 = P(A) → A and B are independent.
P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A) in general — confusing them is the "prosecutor's fallacy".

Total probability theorem

If B₁, B₂, …, Bn are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (they split S), then for any event A: P(A) = P(B₁)P(A | B₁) + P(B₂)P(A | B₂) + … + P(Bn)P(A | Bn)
Overall defect rate of a factory with several machines; overall pass rate across sections.
Draw a tree diagram: first branches for the B's, then branches for A given each B; multiply along branches and add the ones ending in A.
The B's must cover all possibilities without overlap.

3.5 Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem (inverse probability)

Proposed by Rev. Thomas Bayes (1763). It reverses a conditional probability: from P(A | Bi) (easy to know) it finds P(Bi | A) (what we want). It updates prior beliefs with new evidence.
P(Bi | A) = P(Bi) · P(A | Bi)Σj P(Bj) · P(A | Bj)

P(Bi) = prior probability · P(A | Bi) = likelihood · P(Bi | A) = posterior probability

Medical diagnosis, spam filters (P(spam | words)), fraud detection, which machine produced a defective item, machine learning (Naïve Bayes classifier), updating forecasts.
  1. List the causes B₁…Bn with priors P(Bi).
  2. Write the likelihoods P(A | Bi).
  3. Multiply: joint P(Bi)P(A | Bi) for each i.
  4. Add them: P(A) (total probability).
  5. Posterior = joint ÷ total. Posteriors add to 1.
Needs reliable priors; results can be counter-intuitive (rare diseases); assumes the causes are exhaustive and mutually exclusive.

1 % of a population has a disease. A test detects it 99 % of the time (sensitivity) but also gives a positive result for 5 % of healthy people. If a person tests positive, what is the probability they have the disease?

P(D | +) = (0.01 × 0.99) / (0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05) = 0.0099 / (0.0099 + 0.0495) = 0.0099/0.0594 = 0.1667.

3.6 Random variables, expectation and variance

Random variable

A random variable X assigns a number to each outcome of a random experiment. Discrete random variables take countable values (number of heads, defective items, customers per hour); continuous ones take any value in an interval (height, time, weight).
Turning business uncertainty into numbers: demand next week, number of claims, time to failure.
The probability distribution lists each value with its probability. For discrete X this is the probability mass function p(x) with Σp(x) = 1; for continuous X it is a probability density function f(x) with total area 1 (probabilities are areas).
E(X) = μ = Σ x·p(x)      Var(X) = σ² = Σ x²·p(x) − μ²
Example: X = number on a die: E(X) = (1 + 2 + … + 6)/6 = 3.5; E(X²) = 91/6; Var = 91/6 − 12.25 = 2.917.
Business example: a deal earns ₹100 with probability 0.2, ₹20 with 0.5, and loses ₹30 with 0.3. E = 100(0.2) + 20(0.5) − 30(0.3) = 20 + 10 − 9 = ₹21 per deal.
The expected value is a long-run average — a single outcome can be very different.

Probability distributions — overview

A theoretical distribution is a formula that describes how probabilities are spread over the values of X. Three are in the syllabus:
Choosing the right model: count of successes in n trials → Binomial; count of rare events in an interval → Poisson; continuous measurements → Normal.

3.7 Binomial distribution

Definition and conditions

Developed by James Bernoulli. Gives the probability of exactly r successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, each with success probability p (failure q = 1 − p).
P(X = r) = nCr pr qn−r,   r = 0, 1, …, n     Mean = np,   Variance = npq,   SD = √(npq)
Conditions: (1) fixed number of trials n; (2) each trial has only two outcomes; (3) p is constant from trial to trial; (4) trials are independent.
Number of defective items in a sample of 10; number of customers (out of 20 visitors) who buy; number of heads in coin tosses; number of correct guesses in a multiple-choice test; quality-control sampling.
Properties: discrete; mean > variance (since q < 1); symmetric when p = q = ½, positively skewed when p < ½, negatively skewed when p > ½; the probabilities are the terms of (q + p)n.
Requires constant p and independence — sampling without replacement from a small lot violates this; tedious for large n (use Poisson or Normal approximation).
  1. 10 coins tossed. P(exactly 6 heads)? n = 10, p = q = ½. P(6) = 10C6 (½)10 = 210/1024 = 0.2051.
  2. P(at least 8 heads)? P(8) + P(9) + P(10) = (45 + 10 + 1)/1024 = 56/1024 = 0.0547.
  3. 10 % of items are defective. In a sample of 5, P(no defective)? 0.95 = 0.5905. P(at most 1) = 0.5905 + 5(0.1)(0.9)4 = 0.5905 + 0.3281 = 0.9185. Mean = 0.5, variance = 0.45.
  4. Mean 4 and variance 3. Find n and p. npq/np = q = 3/4 → p = 1/4; n = 4/(1/4) = 16.

3.8 Poisson distribution

Definition and conditions

Developed by S.D. Poisson (1837). Models the number of times a rare event occurs in a fixed interval of time, space or volume. It is the limit of the Binomial when n is large, p is small and m = np is moderate.
P(X = r) = e−m mrr!,   r = 0, 1, 2, …     Mean = Variance = m    (e ≈ 2.71828)
Use when n ≥ 20 and p ≤ 0.05 (rule of thumb), or when only the average rate m is known.
Number of accidents per day on a highway, calls to a call centre per minute, typing errors per page, defects per metre of cloth, customers arriving at an ATM per hour, server requests per second.
Properties: discrete; only one parameter m; mean = variance; positively skewed (more symmetric as m grows); recurrence P(r + 1) = P(r) × m/(r + 1) makes calculation fast.
Values of e−m: m = 0.5 → 0.6065; 1 → 0.3679; 1.5 → 0.2231; 2 → 0.1353; 3 → 0.0498; 4 → 0.0183; 5 → 0.0067.
Assumes events occur independently at a constant average rate; not suitable when events cluster.
  1. On average 2 accidents per day. P(no accident)? e−2 = 0.1353. P(exactly 2) = 0.1353 × 2²/2! = 0.2707. P(at least one) = 1 − 0.1353 = 0.8647.
  2. 2 % of fuses are defective. In a box of 100, P(at most 2 defective)? m = np = 100 × 0.02 = 2. P(0) + P(1) + P(2) = e−2(1 + 2 + 2) = 5 × 0.1353 = 0.6767.
  3. If P(X = 1) = P(X = 2), find the mean. e−mm = e−mm²/2 → m = 2.

3.9 Normal distribution

Definition

The most important continuous distribution (de Moivre, Laplace, Gauss) — the familiar bell curve. Many natural and business measurements are approximately normal because they are the sum of many small independent effects (Central Limit Theorem).
f(x) = 1σ√(2π) e−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)      Z = X − μσ ~ N(0, 1)
Probabilities are areas under the curve. We convert X to the standard normal variate Z (mean 0, SD 1) and use the Z-table, which gives the area between 0 and z.
Heights, weights, exam marks, measurement errors, product dimensions in quality control, returns on investments (approx.), IQ scores; basis of confidence intervals and many tests.
Properties:
  1. Bell-shaped and symmetric about μ; mean = median = mode.
  2. Total area = 1; area on each side of μ = 0.5.
  3. Tails approach but never touch the x-axis (asymptotic).
  4. Determined completely by μ and σ.
  5. Empirical rule: μ ± 1σ covers 68.27 %, μ ± 2σ covers 95.45 %, μ ± 3σ covers 99.73 %.
  6. Points of inflection at μ ± σ; quartile deviation ≈ 0.6745σ.
Steps: (1) compute z = (x − μ)/σ; (2) read area from the table; (3) combine areas using symmetry (area beyond z = 0.5 − table value; between two z of opposite signs = sum; same sign = difference).
Real data may be skewed (incomes) or have heavy tails (stock crashes); cannot have negative values for quantities like price, though the model allows them.
  1. P(X > 75): z = (75 − 60)/10 = 1.5. Area 0 to 1.5 = 0.4332. P = 0.5 − 0.4332 = 0.0668. Out of 1000 students, about 67 score above 75.
  2. P(50 < X < 70): z = −1 and +1. Area = 0.3413 + 0.3413 = 0.6826.
  3. P(X < 45): z = −1.5 → by symmetry 0.5 − 0.4332 = 0.0668.
  4. P(65 < X < 80): z = 0.5 and 2.0 → 0.4772 − 0.1915 = 0.2857.
  5. Top 10 % cut-off: area 0.40 from 0 → z ≈ 1.28 → x = 60 + 1.28 × 10 = 72.8.

3.10 Estimation and hypothesis testing

Population, sample, parameter, statistic

A population is the whole group of interest (all customers); a sample is the part we actually observe. A parameter describes the population (μ, σ, p) and is usually unknown; a statistic describes the sample (x̄, s, p̂) and is used to estimate it. The standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution is its standard error: S.E.(x̄) = σ/√n.
A survey of 500 voters estimates the support of millions; testing 50 bulbs estimates the life of the whole production.
Point estimate: a single value (x̄ = 52 estimates μ). Interval estimate (confidence interval): a range likely to contain μ. Large sample: x̄ ± zα/2 · σ/√n     Small sample (σ unknown): x̄ ± tα/2, n−1 · s/√n Example: n = 36, x̄ = 50, σ = 6 → 95 % CI = 50 ± 1.96 × 6/6 = 48.04 to 51.96.
Properties of a good estimator: unbiasedness, consistency, efficiency, sufficiency.
A 95 % interval means 95 % of such intervals contain μ — not that μ has a 95 % chance of being in this one.

Hypothesis testing: the vocabulary

A hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter, tested with sample data.
  • Null hypothesis H₀: "no difference / no effect" (μ = 100; the two means are equal; attributes are independent).
  • Alternative hypothesis H₁: what we suspect (μ ≠ 100 two-tailed; μ > 100 or μ < 100 one-tailed).
  • Level of significance α: the risk of rejecting a true H₀ we accept — usually 5 % (0.05) or 1 %.
  • Test statistic: t, F, χ², z computed from the data.
  • Critical value / critical region: from the table at α and the degrees of freedom; if the statistic falls in the critical (rejection) region, reject H₀.
  • Degrees of freedom (df): number of values free to vary after constraints (n − 1 for one sample).
  • p-value: probability of a result at least this extreme if H₀ were true; reject H₀ if p < α.
Is a new fertiliser better? Do three shops have the same average sales? Is gender related to product preference?
Five steps for every test (write them in the exam):
  1. State H₀ and H₁.
  2. Choose α (5 %) and identify df.
  3. Compute the test statistic.
  4. Find the table (critical) value.
  5. Decision: if |calculated| < table → accept (fail to reject) H₀; if greater → reject H₀. Conclude in words.
Two kinds of errors are always possible: Type I (reject a true H₀, probability α) and Type II (accept a false H₀, probability β). Reducing one increases the other for fixed n.
DecisionH₀ trueH₀ false

3.11 The t-test

Student's t-distribution and when to use it

W.S. Gosset, writing as "Student" (1908, Guinness brewery), showed that when the sample is small (n < 30) and the population σ is unknown, the standardised mean follows a t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom — bell-shaped like the normal but with fatter tails; it approaches the normal as df grows.
Testing a claimed mean (one-sample), comparing two independent groups (two-sample), comparing before/after measurements (paired).

1. One sample: t = x̄ − μS / √n,   S = √[Σ(X − x̄)²/(n − 1)],   df = n − 1

2. Two independent samples: t = x̄₁ − x̄₂S √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂),   S = √{[Σ(X₁ − x̄₁)² + Σ(X₂ − x̄₂)²] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)},   df = n₁ + n₂ − 2

3. Paired: d = after − before; t = d̄Sd / √n,   Sd = √[Σ(d − d̄)²/(n − 1)],   df = n − 1

Note: some books use the sample SD s computed with n and write t = (x̄ − μ)√(n − 1)/s — it gives the same t.

Assumptions: random samples, approximately normal population, equal variances (for the pooled two-sample test), interval/ratio data.

Sensitive to outliers and strong skewness in small samples; only compares one or two means (for three or more use ANOVA).

3.12 Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

One-way ANOVA

Developed by R.A. Fisher. Tests whether three or more population means are equal. Instead of many t-tests (which inflates the Type I error), it splits the total variation into variation between groups (due to the factor) and within groups (random error), and compares them with the F-ratio: F = variance between samples (MSC)variance within samples (MSE) If the group means are equal, both estimate the same variance and F ≈ 1; a large F means the means differ.
Comparing average sales of 4 stores, yields of 3 wheat varieties, scores of students taught by 3 methods, customer satisfaction across 5 branches.

Assumptions: samples are random and independent; populations are normal; variances are equal (homoscedasticity).

  1. H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μk; H₁: not all means are equal.
  2. T = grand total of all N observations; correction factor CF = T²/N.
  3. SST (total) = ΣX² − CF.
  4. SSC (between columns/groups) = Σ(Tj²/nj) − CF, where Tj is the total of group j.
  5. SSE (within/error) = SST − SSC.
  6. Degrees of freedom: between = k − 1; within = N − k; total = N − 1.
  7. Mean squares: MSC = SSC/(k − 1); MSE = SSE/(N − k); F = MSC/MSE.
  8. Compare with F-table at (k − 1, N − k) df; F calculated > F table → reject H₀.
Tells you that some means differ, not which ones (post-hoc tests such as Tukey's are needed); sensitive to unequal variances.

3.13 Chi-square (χ²) test

The χ² statistic

Developed by Karl Pearson (1900). Compares observed frequencies (O) with expected frequencies (E) under a hypothesis: χ² = Σ (O − E)²E χ² = 0 means perfect agreement; the larger χ², the bigger the discrepancy. It is a non-parametric test (makes no assumption about the population distribution) used for counts / categorical data.
Goodness of fit (does data follow a given distribution — is a die fair?); test of independence (is gender related to product preference? is vaccination related to infection?); test of homogeneity.

Test of independence (contingency table):

  1. H₀: the two attributes are independent.
  2. Expected frequency of each cell: E = (Row total × Column total) / Grand total.
  3. Compute (O − E)²/E for each cell and add → χ².
  4. df = (r − 1)(c − 1).
  5. Compare with table χ² at α; calculated > table → reject H₀ (attributes are associated).

Goodness of fit: E from the hypothesised distribution; df = (number of classes − 1 − number of estimated parameters).

Conditions: random sample; observations independent; total N ≥ 50; every expected frequency ≥ 5 (pool small classes); data are frequencies, not percentages.

Only tells whether an association exists, not its strength or direction; unreliable when expected frequencies are small (for 2 × 2 tables use Yates' correction: replace |O − E| by |O − E| − 0.5).
