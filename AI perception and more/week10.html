Distributions, Knowledge Distillation, Contrastive Learning, Multi-Modal Fusion, Pruning & Continual Learning

Networks output probability distributions, and losses compare distributions. That view leads to distilling a large teacher into a small, real-time student, and to learning features without labels through contrastive learning. The second half applies these tools to drones: fusing RGB with infrared when a sensor may fail, pruning channels, learning new tasks without forgetting old ones, and segmenting classes described in plain language.

10.1 Networks as probability distributions

A probability distribution assigns a likelihood to every outcome of a random variable. Discrete: a probability mass function P(x) with Σx P(x) = 1. Continuous: a probability density function p(x) with ∫ p(x) dx = 1.
Almost every ML task models a distribution. Classification models P(y | x) over labels; generative modelling models P(x) over the data itself (images, text, audio). Every softmax at the end of a network produces a probability distribution, not just a list of scores.
Two views of what a network learns. In the function-approximation view, fθ(x) approximates a target mapping. In the distribution view, the network learns Pθ(y | x) or Pθ(x, y), and training pulls that distribution towards the true (empirical) data distribution. So the loss function is a distribution-comparison tool, and training a network means minimising a distance between distributions. Intermediate activations across a batch also form empirical distributions, which is what distillation exploits.

10.2 Measuring the gap: KL divergence, MMD, Wasserstein

KL divergence: DKL(P‖Q) = Σx P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)) (continuous: ∫ p log(p/q) dx).
Cross-entropy: H(Pdata, Pθ) = DKL(Pdata‖Pθ) + H(Pdata). The data entropy is a constant, so minimising cross-entropy = minimising KL.
MMD: MMD²(P, Q) = ‖Ex∼P[φ(x)] − Ey∼Q[φ(y)]‖²ℋ, where k(x, x′) = ⟨φ(x), φ(x′)⟩ℋ.

The lecture also lists the Wasserstein distance (the "earth mover's" cost of moving probability mass from P to Q). Like MMD, it stays meaningful when the distributions don't overlap, which is why it is used for GAN training in Week 11.

DKL(P‖Q) averages over P. Wherever P(x) > 0 but Q(x) → 0, the term log(P/Q) → ∞. Putting the teacher first, DKL(PT‖PS), is mass-covering: the student is punished heavily for giving near-zero probability to any class the teacher thinks plausible. Example: P = [0.5, 0.5], Q = [0.9, 0.1] gives DKL(P‖Q) = 0.511 nats but DKL(Q‖P) = 0.368 nats.

10.3 Knowledge distillation (teacher → student)

Motivation. Speech and object recognition must extract structure from very large, highly redundant datasets, which may need a large model, but deployment (a drone!) must run in real time. Distillation is a different kind of training that transfers the knowledge of the large trained teacher into a small student that is suitable for deployment.
Soft targets with temperature. pi = exp(zi/T) / Σj exp(zj/T). At T = 1 the teacher is nearly one-hot. A higher T softens the distribution and reveals the "dark knowledge": the relative similarity between wrong classes (a "2" looks more like a "3" than a "7"). Hard labels carry none of this. A typical loss is α·CE(y, pS) + (1 − α)·T²·DKL(pT(T)‖pS(T)). The T² factor keeps the soft-target gradients on the same scale as T changes.
What to match? The lecture lists three options:
  • Output logits (soft targets).
  • Intermediate features. The student mimics the distribution of the teacher's hidden activations (Neuron Selectivity Transfer). FitNets "hints" compare teacher and student layers after a linear transformation to match dimensions. Attention maps transfer too: activation statistics correlate spatially with the predicted objects, and stronger networks have sharper attention peaks (Network-in-Network 62%, ResNet-34 73%, ResNet-101 77.3% ImageNet accuracy).
  • Gradients. ∂L/∂x highlights the input pixels with the highest importance (a gradient-based spatial attention map).

10.4 Contrastive learning: CPC, SimCLR, MoCo, CMC

Learn representations by comparing pairs of samples rather than fitting labels. Pull together the embeddings of positives (two augmented views of the same image) and push apart the negatives (views of different images). No labels are needed: the supervisory signal comes from the data's own structure.
CPC (Contrastive Predictive Coding, van den Oord et al. 2018) predicts a future latent representation from past context. It discriminates the true future embedding from distractor (negative) embeddings instead of reconstructing raw future data, so effort goes into high-level, predictable structure rather than pixel-level noise. CPC introduced the InfoNCE loss:
ℒ = −log [ exp(sim(z, z+)/τ) / Σi=1..K exp(sim(z, zi)/τ) ]
Here z+ is the positive, the zi range over K candidates (one positive plus negatives), sim is cosine similarity or a dot product, and τ is the temperature. It is a softmax cross-entropy where the "correct class" is the positive.

SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020)

Two augmentations t, t′ ∼ 𝒯 sampled from the same family are applied to each example → two correlated views. These pass through the base encoder f(·) (representation h) and then a small projection head g(·) (z), where the NT-Xent loss (normalised-temperature InfoNCE) is applied over a minibatch of N examples (2N views). After pretraining, g is discarded and h is used downstream. The contrastive loss makes z invariant to augmentations (colour, crop…), which throws away information. g absorbs that loss, so h keeps richer features. SimCLR depends on very large batches to get many negatives.

MoCo (He et al., 2020)

Momentum Contrast removes SimCLR's large-batch dependence. It keeps a queue (dictionary) of negative embeddings from previous batches and encodes keys with a momentum-averaged encoder (θk ← m·θk + (1 − m)·θq), so the queued keys stay consistent. The number of negatives is decoupled from the batch size.

Contrastive Multiview Coding (CMC, Tian et al. 2020). The supervisory signal is the natural co-occurrence of several views of the same scene. Views of the same scene map to nearby points, and views of different scenes map far apart. For two views V1, V2, two encoders fθ1, fθ2 extract compact latents, and a critic hθ(·) is trained to score congruent (positive) pairs high and incongruent (negative) pairs low.
This extends directly to multispectral remote sensing: different spectral bands act as different views of the same scene, giving self-supervised pre-training without labels.

10.5 Pixel-level contrastive learning for segmentation

Segmentation networks mine only local context: each pixel's embedding uses its surroundings, and per-pixel cross-entropy compares a pixel only with its own label. They ignore the global context of the whole dataset.
Pixel contrast (Wang et al., ICCV 2021): for an anchor pixel embedding i, distinguish a positive (a pixel of the same class, from any image) from negatives (pixels of other classes) in a projected embedding space, using an InfoNCE loss with dot products and temperature τ. This builds a well-structured pixel embedding space from the global semantic similarities among labelled pixels across images. Many negatives are needed, so a large external memory bank stores pixel embeddings beyond the mini-batch.
Sampling strategies. Hardest-example sampling: for each anchor, take only the top-K hardest negatives and positives from the memory bank. Semi-hard sampling: collect the top 10% nearest negatives and the 10% farthest positives.

10.6 Multi-modal perception: RGB + IR/SAR

Perception is fundamental to robotics (detection, visual SLAM, segmentation), and a perception failure propagates errors through the whole system. RGB-only perception fails in low light and adverse weather (e.g. a model trained on sunny Cityscapes breaks at night). Complementary sensors help: infrared (IR) and SAR. Domain gaps come from sim-to-real, from geography, weather and illumination, and from different sensors.
Multi-modal perception learns complementary information. It usually assumes calibrated / co-registered images (RGB, depth, event, LiDAR…) and must also cope with sim-to-real, missing-modality / sensor-failure scenarios, and unseen target domains.
Key insight for multi-spectral systems. Different imaging systems observe the same object. Low frequencies capture shared information (modality-shared features: shallow layers and the convolutional layers of the backbone). High frequencies carry each modality's distinct features (modality-specific features: deeper layers and the BatchNorm layers). Leveraging the correlation between low and high frequencies constrains the solution space: shared structure anchors the alignment while modality-specific detail is preserved.

Channel Exchange Network (CEN, Wang et al. 2022). The modality branches share convolutional weights but keep distinct BatchNorm layers per modality. A channel whose BN scaling factor γ is below a threshold is "inhibited": it contributes little, so its feature map is replaced by the other modality's feature map at the same position. This is parameter-free fusion built on the network-pruning philosophy: the smallest γ has the least impact on the output.

10.8 Missing-modality segmentation: SKD-Net and OGP-Net

Problem formulation. The goal is to bridge the cross-modal gap between RGB and IR for semantic segmentation. Training uses co-registered RGB–IR pairs from dataset D with pixel-wise labels Y, and the model outputs a segmentation mask S with parameters θ. At inference, the multi-modal setting feeds both images, and the missing-modality setting (night, low light, failed camera) feeds only IR.
These are knowledge-distillation methods from the instructors' lab (Sikdar, Teotia & Sundaram): SKD-Net (Spectral-based KD, ICRA 2024) and OGP-Net (Optically-Guided Pixel-level contrastive learning, AAAI 2025). Step 1 pre-trains an RGB teacher. Step 2 trains OGP-Net (the student) with pixel-level distillation from that teacher, using two components:
  • DMC (Distillation with Multi-view Contrastive) combines (i) feature exchange, (ii) pixel-level knowledge distillation and (iii) multi-view contrastive learning. It projects semantics from both modalities into a unified latent space as multi-view feature maps (MVFM) and applies contrastive learning on them. This removes the need for extra augmentations or explicit views from other sensors, and balances intra- and inter-modal learning.
  • DUR preserves modality-specific representations within the latent space.
Results. OGP-Net (IR only) beats the IR baseline by 4.81%, and OGP-Net (RGB-IR) beats the RGB baseline by 5.64%. The ablation on MSRS shows the best setting is shared conv layers + unshared BatchNorm + feature exchange, the channel-exchange philosophy.

10.9 Pruning: network slimming and its limits

Network slimming (Liu et al., ICCV 2017) puts an L1 penalty on the BatchNorm scaling factors γ during training, so unimportant channels are pushed towards zero. Channels with the smallest γ (or smallest weight norm) are then pruned and the slimmer network is fine-tuned. The result is fewer parameters and FLOPs and faster inference on embedded drone hardware.
"Smaller-norm-less-informative" is only a heuristic (Ye et al., ICLR 2018). A small γ does not guarantee zero contribution, because later layers can rescale it and channels interact. Each removed channel deletes some representational capacity, so pruning more and more channels eventually hurts accuracy, especially without fine-tuning. A pruning ratio must be chosen and validated.

10.10 Continual (incremental / lifelong) learning

Intelligent systems must adapt to dynamic environments. Continual learning moves from capturing a static data distribution to learning from dynamic ones. The major challenge is catastrophic forgetting: adapting to a new distribution sharply reduces the ability to handle the old ones.
Stability–plasticity trade-off. Too much plasticity (learning new tasks) interferes with stability (remembering old ones), and vice versa. Updates should also be resource-efficient, ideally close to training only on the new samples. Because labels are expensive and scarce, continual learning must also work in few-shot, semi-supervised and even unsupervised settings (e.g. few-shot continual learning: many samples for the base classes, then novel classes with only a few each).
Formal set-up. A batch of task t is Dt,b = {Xt,b, Yt,b}, with task identity t ∈ {1, …, k} and batch index b. A task is defined by its distribution Dt := p(Xt, Yt). The objective is p(D1:k | θ) = Πt=1k p(Dt | θ), but when learning task k the old sets D1 … Dk−1 are inaccessible. A flatter converged loss landscape gives a balanced solution that generalises better across the task sequence.

Freezing all weights after task 1 gives zero forgetting (perfect stability) but zero plasticity: the network can no longer adapt to new tasks. Continual learning is about the balance, not either extreme.

Class-incremental segmentation (e.g. VISTA-CLIP, CVPRW 2025): at step t the labels cover only the current classes Ct, with past and future classes unlabelled. After each step the model must segment all of C1:t, avoiding forgetting of C1:t−1 (stability) while learning Ct (plasticity). Findings: VLMs like CLIP are not great for panoptic segmentation as-is, the CLIP text encoder works well with other vision backbones, and prompts help continual learning.

10.11 Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation and prompt learning

Closed-set segmentation fixes the class set for both training and inference. In open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS), an image I comes with candidate classes C = {C1, …, CN}, each described in natural language. A zero-shot model assigns one Ci to each pixel. N can vary at inference (different downstream tasks), and the model may never have seen the candidate classes in training.
OVSS is built on CLIP vision–language backbones and comes in two architecture types: mask-proposal networks (propose masks, then classify each against the text embeddings) and cost-aggregation models (CAT-Seg: aggregate the image–text similarity cost volume). Out-of-domain generalisation is tested on the MESS multi-domain benchmark.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning of VLMs optimises a few task-specific learnable tokens: visual prompt tuning (VPT) injects trainable prompt tokens into the visual encoder's input sequence across several Transformer layers; text prompt learning refines contextual word embeddings with learnable vectors; multi-modal prompts do both. The lab's CoLoRA (IROS 2026) combines optimal transport with LoRA for multi-domain OVSS. The AetherVision-Bench benchmark covers open-vocabulary RGB-IR segmentation across aerial and ground views.
