12.1 The perimeter defence problem

Motivation. Autonomous UAVs serve logistics, defence, and search & reconnaissance, but they raise privacy, safety and security concerns. A freely moving intruder UAV can collect information about critical infrastructure, so a protected territory needs a team of defender UAVs operating inside it, with cooperation, coordination and collaboration. Other drivers of collaborative autonomy: urban intervention, forest-fire fighting, AI for dogfights.
Related problems:
  • Guarding problem (Isaacs, differential games): capture the evader before it enters the territory; 1 vs 1 or 2 vs 1; the guard can operate outside the territory.
  • Reach–avoid: an n vs m multiplayer game with points for intruding.
  • Earlier perimeter-defence work (Shishika & Kumar): defenders move on the perimeter; more defenders than intruders; each defender assigned to at most one intruder; both are eliminated on capture.
Research gaps addressed: scalability (a decentralised solution), dynamic intruders (new intruders appear later), distributed sensing and partial observability (each defender senses independently), and sequential capture (one defender captures several intruders in turn).

Problem set-up.

  • Objective: capture intruders before they enter the territory.
  • Defenders have sensors of radius Rs and share information with neighbours by synchronous communication.
  • NI > ND (more intruders than defenders) and VD,max > VI,max.
  • Intruders may be non-manoeuvring or manoeuvring.
  • Neutralisation: a defender captures an intruder when the intruder comes within an ε-vicinity (a small capture ball) of the defender.

12.2 Spatio-temporal multi-task assignment and DREAM

Defenders operate on and inside the territory. From an intruder's position, velocity and heading, compute its arrival location pTi (where it will cross the boundary) and time of arrival ti. Each intruder becomes a spatio-temporal task Ti(pTi, ti). A defender neutralises it by being at that location at that time.
Optimisation: min Σi,j cfij δfij + Σk,j cskj δskj with binary δ. Here δf means defender i does task j first, and δs means task j is done immediately after task k.
  • First-task cost: cfij = α‖pTj − pDi‖ if ‖pTj − pDi‖/Vmax ≤ tj, otherwise κ.
  • Subsequent-task cost: cskj = α‖pTj − pTk‖ if tmink,j ≤ tj − tk; κ if tmink,j > tj − tk > 0; and ∞ if tj ≤ tk. Here tmink,j = ‖pTj − pTk‖/Vmax.
  • Constraints: each task is executed exactly once; each defender does at most one first task; each task has at most one immediate successor.
Infeasible pairs get a prohibitive cost (the assignment answer: infinity), so the optimiser never chooses them while a feasible option exists.
DREAM (Dynamic REsource Allocation with Multi-task assignment): the given defenders may not be able to neutralise all intruders because of the spatio-temporal nature of the tasks, so reserve defenders are used. It is a non-iterative two-step method:
  1. Solve the assignment, count the time-infeasible assignments q, and add q reserve defenders.
  2. Re-solve; now every assignment is feasible.
It is scalable, online-computable, simple, and uses the minimum number of defenders.
Theorem 1: adding one reserve makes exactly one previously infeasible intruder feasible. Corollary: q reserves are necessary and sufficient. Theorem 2: with Euclidean costs, the optimal DREAM solution gives collision-free trajectories.

12.3 Learning the assignment with a spiking neural network

Optimisation must be re-solved for every configuration and can grow combinatorially. A learning-based approach must also handle uncertainty: changes in the territory's shape, in intruder velocities and in the defender team. Spiking neural networks efficiently handle sparse spatio-temporal events: information is carried by the timing of spikes, which also suits low-power neuromorphic hardware.
Spike input generation: the perimeter is segmented into zones, which makes the approach generalisable to shape changes. The input is a spike train representing the presence of intruders (and defenders) and their proportional distance/time to the perimeter. A change in intruder velocity shows up as a change in spike time.
Multitask assignment SNN (DeALS and related work):
  • Input layer: 2m neurons (m = observable zones) carrying the defender and intruder spikes.
  • SEFRON layer: 2m spiking neurons. If a defender should be assigned to zone j, output f2j−1 is trained to spike before f2j (and vice versa).
  • Binary output: 1 = assigned, 0 = unassigned.
Results: mean success rate over 1000 runs; in the partial-observation scenario the defender observes 41.67% of the area.
Generalisability:
  • The input encoding does not depend on the perimeter's shape.
  • Heterogeneous intruders are handled because a velocity change only shifts spike times.
  • An SNN trained on the unit circle with 5 defenders still works when the radius grows to 1.5× and when the team size changes, with no retraining.

12.4 Sensor-denied navigation: where am I?

The traditional autonomous-vehicle architecture starts with localisation. Its sensors are either:
  • Intrinsic (proprioceptive): IMU (accelerometers + gyroscopes), GPS, flow, pitot.
  • Extrinsic: camera, LiDAR, radar, IR.
Traditionally GNSS + IMU are fused (Kalman filter) to estimate velocity and attitude. GNSS-denied navigation is crucial for UAVs because GNSS jamming and spoofing are now common.
Alternatives:
  • LiDAR localisation by scan matching (FAST-LIO2).
  • Vision via optical flow, feature matching or visual place recognition (AnyLoc).
  • Thermal cameras.
These extrinsic sensors are vulnerable to environmental conditions. The spectrum of solutions runs from intrinsic (IMU + GNSS, IMU + altitude, data-driven IMU) to extrinsic (visual, LiDAR, radar and Gaussian-splatting SLAM). A data-driven IMU approach is low-cost and works on aerial vehicles.
Challenges of inertial systems: bias estimation, drift prediction and time synchronisation. Adaptive switching between sources brings its own problems: fusing multiple receivers and computational complexity.

Shared challenges: loop closure (revisit detection), long-term operation (seasonal and map ageing), drift and error accumulation, dynamic scenes (the moving-world problem).

  • RaI-SLAM (radar–inertial): removes dynamic objects using only Doppler velocities and uses a dual-factor tightly coupled graph. Limits: it needs extensive filtering of radar data, and its place-recognition threshold δd is a fixed prior, which can fail.
  • R3LIVE++: triple fusion of LiDAR + camera + IMU, where VIO and LIO share one map, producing a coloured radiance map. Limits: no loop closure, and if either LIO or VIO fails the model diverges.
  • WildGS-SLAM (CVPR 2025): monocular 3D Gaussian-splatting SLAM that learns an online uncertainty mask to down-weight dynamic pixels, using rendering losses only. Fast camera motion and clutter make rendering and matching hard. The lab's variant adds multi-view feature consistency to train a teacher and distils a student that gives spatio-temporally consistent masks. ATE improves 3.1 → 2.8 cm and 1.3 → 1.2 cm on TUM sequences.
  • DROID-Splat: end-to-end SLAM + 3D Gaussian splatting. It handles static scenes and short-to-medium sequences, but colour becomes inaccurate and distorted on long ones.

12.7 The future: end-to-end navigation and foundation models

Today's pipeline processes sensor input through separate blocks (perception → mapping → planning → control), and errors propagate between them. End-to-end navigation uses integrated AI models (foundation models, Vision-Language-Action models) that convert sensor inputs (images, LiDAR) directly into safe trajectories and control commands.
Four priority research directions:
  1. Long-context backbones: state-space models (Mamba) for inertial odometry over long horizons without t² drift.
  2. Physics-informed learning: ODE and Lie-group constraints embedded in the training loss.
  3. End-to-end pose regression: loop closure learned inside the model, which outputs pose, not just velocity.
  4. Multi-modal fusion: the IMU co-trained with camera, LiDAR and radar in a shared navigation embedding.
